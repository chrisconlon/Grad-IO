\section{The problem}

\begin{frame}[plain]{Conduct Testing in Industrial Organization}
Foundational Empirical IO Question: How do we observe data on price and quantity and infer which model of firm behavior generated those outcomes?

\begin{itemize}
\item Early work: Porter (1983), Bresnahan (1982,1987)
\item Subsequent work defined the ``menu" approach: Nevo (1998, 2001), Villas-Boas (2007)
\item Recent revival of ``internalization'' parameters: Miller and Weinberg (2017), Crawford, Lee, Whinston, and Yurukoglu (2017), Pakes (2017)
\item Parallel work by: Duarte, Magnolfi, S\o lvsten, Sullivan (QE 2024): which test is best (RV) and when instruments are too weak to test. Magnolfi, Quint, Sullivan, Waldfogel (2022): should we test or estimate?
\item Applications of our test: Starc and Wollmann (2022), Scuderi and Roussille (2025), others?
\item Today: the classic approaches; the supply conditional moment restriction (CMR) every test is built on; the Backus--Conlon--Sinkinson scalar test (``BCS 1.0'') and the full-CMR test (``BCS 2.0''); the common-ownership application.
\end{itemize}
Is conduct testable? Berry and Haile (2014): yes.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Conduct Testing in Industrial Organization}
\begin{itemize}
\item Absent additional restrictions, we cannot generally look at data on $(P,Q)$ and decide whether or not collusion is taking place.
\begin{itemize}
\item You say we started colluding at date $t$, I say we received a correlated shock to $mc$.
\end{itemize}
\item We can make progress in two ways: (1) parametric restrictions on marginal costs; (2) exclusion restrictions on supply.
\begin{itemize}
\item Most of the literature focuses on (1): a functional form for marginal cost in observed cost shifters plus an unobserved shock.
\item In principle (2) is possible if we have instruments that shift demand for products but not supply. (These are much easier to come up with than ``supply shifters''). (This is the point Berry Haile 2014 make).
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conduct Is a Markup: Profit Weights in the Bertrand FOC}
\small
Firm $f$ puts weight $\kappa_{fg}$ on rival $g$'s profits ($\kappa_{ff} = 1$):
\begin{align*}
\max_{\{p_j\}_{j \in \calJ_f}} \sum_{j \in \calJ_f} (p_j - c_j)\, q_j(\symbf{p}) + \alert{\sum_{g \neq f} \kappa_{fg}} \sum_{k \in \calJ_g} (p_k - c_k)\, q_k(\symbf{p})
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\big(\calH(\kappa) \odot \Delta\big)(\symbf{p} - \symbf{mc}) = \symbf{q},
\end{align*}
with $\Delta$ the demand-derivative matrix of the pricing lecture and $\calH(\kappa)_{(j,k)} = \kappa_{fg}$ for $j \in \calJ_f$, $k \in \calJ_g$.
\begin{itemize}
\item A conduct model $m$ (an $\calH$ matrix, or Cournot, or anything else) maps demand into a \alert{markup} vector,
$\symbf{\eta}^m \equiv \symbf{p} - \symbf{mc} = \big(\calH(\kappa_m) \odot \Delta\big)^{-1} \symbf{q}$.
\item Mergers change $0$'s to $1$'s in $\calH$. Partial coordination is $\kappa_{fg} \in (0,1)$.
\item $\kappa_{fg} > 0$ is not necessarily evidence of malfeasance, just a deviation from \alert{static Bertrand pricing} (Cournot, dynamics, common ownership, \ldots).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[plain]{What is the Common Ownership Hypothesis?}
\begin{itemize}
\item Economics starts from the premise that firms maximize profits.
\begin{itemize}
\item Friedman (1953): natural selection of firms and billiards players.
\item Or, firms answer to investors, \alert{maximize shareholder value}.
\item We generally think of these objectives as aligned (possibly subject to managers' agency frictions).
\end{itemize}
\item So what do investors want?
\begin{itemize}
\item Some (diversified) investors may hold stakes in you \textit{and your competitor}. These are called ``common owners.''
\item Common owners are harmed when managers engage in \alert{business stealing}.
\end{itemize}
\item As a theory of firm behavior in \alert{joint ventures} this is an old idea. The recent innovation is to extend this approach to \alert{passive or institutional investors}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Profit Weights from Portfolios (BCS 2020 P\&P)}
\small
Most models of common ownership (implicitly) assume:
\begin{description}
\item[Investor portfolios] Investor $s$ is entitled to a share $\beta_{fs}$ of firm $f$'s profits: $V_s = \sum_{f} \beta_{fs} \pi_f$.
\item[Manager agency (Rotemberg 1984)] Managers maximize a $\gamma$-weighted average of investor payoffs, $U_f = \sum_{s} \gamma_{fs} V_s$ ($s$ could be the manager herself).
\end{description}
Rearranging,
\begin{align*}
U_f \;\propto\; \pi_{f}+\sum_{g \neq f} \underbrace{\left(\frac{\sum_{s} \gamma_{f s} \beta_{g s}}{\sum_{s} \gamma_{f s} \beta_{f s}}\right)}_{\equiv \kappa_{f g}(\gamma_{f}, \beta)} \pi_{g} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\kappa_{fg}$ is a \alert{profit weight}: a dollar of profit at $g$ is worth $\kappa_{fg}$ dollars to $f$. It is the $\calH(\kappa)$ of the Bertrand FOC, and is not specific to a particular strategic game.
\item $\beta$ (cash-flow rights) is often directly observable; $\gamma$ embeds a model of corporate governance. The literature assumes $\gamma = \beta$ (``proportional control'').
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Reasons for Deviations from Multiproduct Bertrand}
\small
\begin{description}
\item[Biased estimates of own and cross price derivatives:] For anything to work, you need correct estimates of $\Delta$. My prior is most papers \alert{underestimate} diversion ratios for close substitutes.
\item[Vertical Relationships:] Who sets supermarket prices? Just the retailer? Just the manufacturer? Some combination of both? Retailers tend to \alert{soften} downstream price competition.
\item[Faulty Timing Assumptions:] Bertrand is a simultaneous move pricing game. Lots of alternatives (Stackelberg leader-follower, Edgeworth cycles, etc.).
\item[Dynamics and Dynamic Pricing:] Forward looking firms or consumers might not set static Nash prices. [e.g. Temporary Sales, Switching Costs, Network Effects, etc.]
\item[Unmodeled Supergame:] Maybe firms are legally tacitly colluding, higher prices might be about what firms believe will happen in a price war.
\end{description}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Where the Instruments Come From}
\small
In the notation of the demand lectures (characteristics $\textrm{x}$, demand shifters $\textrm{v}$, cost shifters $\textrm{w}$, demographics $\textrm{y}$):
\begin{align*}
\text{demand:}&\quad \delta_{jt}(\symbf{s}_t, \textrm{y}_t; \theta_2) + \alpha p_{jt} = h_d(\textrm{x}_{jt}, \textrm{v}_{jt}) + \xi_{jt}\\
\text{supply, conduct } m:&\quad p_{jt} - \eta^m_{jt}(\symbf{p}_t, \symbf{s}_t; \theta_2) = h_s(\textrm{x}_{jt}, \textrm{w}_{jt}) + \omega^m_{jt}
\end{align*}
\vspace{-0.3cm}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Demand first.} Estimate $\theta_2$ from the demand CMR $\E[\xi_{jt} \mid \textrm{x}_t, \textrm{v}_t, \textrm{w}_t, \textrm{y}_t] = 0$, then compute $\eta^m$ for \alert{every} $m$. The demand CMR is the same for every conduct model: stacking it with supply in one GMM (BLP 1995) only adds common overidentifying restrictions. Conduct enters through the \alert{supply CMR} alone.
\item For any $m$ some $\symbf{mc}$ rationalizes the data ($J$ equations, $J$ unknowns). The supply CMR has content only through \alert{exclusion}: variables that move markups but not own cost (Berry Haile 2014).
\item Candidates: demand shifters $\textrm{v}$ and demographics $\textrm{y}$ (they rotate demand); rivals' characteristics $\textrm{x}_{-j}$ and rivals' cost shifters $\textrm{w}_{-j}$ (they move rivals' prices, hence my markup).
\end{itemize}
\textbf{From here on:} $x$ is every included cost control ($\textrm{x}$, $\textrm{w}$, fixed effects), $z^e$ the excluded instruments, and $\symbf{z} = (z^e, x)$. Demand, and so each $\eta^m$, is taken as known.
\end{frame}


\section{Classic approaches}

\begin{frame}{Three Classic Ways to Use the Supply Restriction}
\small
Each conduct $m$ implies a marginal cost $MC^m = p - \eta^m$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Estimate} (Wald). Put the markup on the right and instrument for it with $z^e$:
$p = h_s(x) + \lambda\, \eta^m + \omega$; test $\lambda = 1$ (Pakes 2017, with Wollmann's trucks). Or estimate $\kappa$ directly. Often underpowered, and $\lambda \neq 1$ is hard to interpret.
\item \textbf{Fit one model} (Anderson--Rubin / GMM-$J$). Put the markup on the left, $MC^m = h_s(x) + \omega^m$, and test $\E[\omega^m \mid \symbf{z}] = 0$. Bonnet and Dubois (2010) and Villas-Boas (2007) compare such fits across models (Rivers--Vuong, Cox), often with no excluded instruments at all.
\item \textbf{Menu} (likelihood ratio). Estimate demand and supply jointly under each $m$ and compare fit (Bresnahan 1987, Nevo 1998).
\end{enumerate}
All three use the same supply CMR. The question is which test to run on it when \alert{every} model is wrong.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Test? Duarte, Magnolfi, S\o lvsten, Sullivan (QE 2024)}
\small
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Specification tests} of one model at a time (Wald on $\lambda=1$, Anderson--Rubin / GMM-$J$, Cox): with enough data \alert{every} model is rejected, because no conduct model is exactly right. Villas-Boas's two-way Cox rejections are the symptom.
\item \textbf{Model selection} (Rivers--Vuong 2002): compare two models' criteria under the null that they are \alert{equally} misspecified. Only RV behaves sensibly when both models are wrong, which is always.
\item The catch: RV is \alert{degenerate} when \alert{neither} model is falsified by the instruments (their Proposition 3). DMSS define \alert{weak instruments for testing} and give an effective-$F$ diagnostic (\texttt{pyRVtest}).
\item Their criterion uses $K$ unconditional moments $\bar{\symbf{m}}_m = \E[z^e\, \omega^m]$ in the 2SLS quadratic form $\bar{\symbf{m}}_m' \,\E[z^e z^{e\prime}]^{-1} \bar{\symbf{m}}_m$: the supply CMR contracted against $K$ directions. BCS 1.0 uses \alert{one} optimal direction; BCS 2.0 uses \alert{all} of them.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Model Selection under Misspecification}

\begin{frame}{The Supply CMR and the Null: Ranking Misspecified Models}
\small
Residualize each model's implied cost on the controls, then ask whether what is left is predictable from the excluded instruments:
\begin{align*}
\omega^m = MC^m - h_s^m(x), \quad h_s^m(x) \equiv \E[MC^m \mid x],
\qquad
\mu^m(\symbf{z}) \equiv \E[\omega^m \mid \symbf{z}],
\qquad
Q(m) \equiv \|\mu^m\|^2 = \E\big[\mu^m(\symbf{z})^2\big] .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item Correct conduct: $\mu^m \equiv 0$. Wrong conduct: $\mu^m \neq 0$, \alert{if} the instruments are rich enough to tell (falsifiability; Berry Haile 2014). $\E[\omega^m \mid x] = 0$ holds by construction for every $m$, so all the content is in $z^e$.
\item On real data every $Q(m) > 0$. The honest hypothesis is not ``Bertrand is true'' but \alert{equal fit} (Vuong 1989, Rivers--Vuong 2002, Chen--Hong--Shum 2007):
$H_0: Q(m_1) = Q(m_2)$ against $H_A$: one model is closer to the CMR.
\item A ``test of conduct'' is therefore a \alert{ranking}; the model confidence set holds the models never significantly beaten, not the correct one.
\item $\mu^m$ is a function. Contract it against one direction (BCS 1.0), $K$ directions (DMSS), or estimate the norm (BCS 2.0). Notation: $\langle a, b \rangle = \E[a(\symbf{z})\, b(\symbf{z})]$, $\|a\|^2 = \langle a, a \rangle$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\section{Backus, Conlon, Sinkinson: the scalar test (BCS 1.0)}

\begin{frame}{One Direction: The Scalar Moment and Rivers--Vuong}
\small
For any direction $g(\symbf{z})$, iterated expectations collapse the CMR to a scalar:
\begin{align*}
\bar m_m = \langle \mu^m, g \rangle = \E[\omega^m\, g(\symbf{z})], \qquad \widetilde Q(m) = \bar m_m^2, \qquad \Delta\widetilde Q = \bar m_1^2 - \bar m_2^2,
\\
\mathsf{T}_{RV} = \frac{\sqrt{n}\, \Delta\widetilde Q}{\widehat\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \text{ under } \Delta\widetilde Q = 0 .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Validity is measurability.} $g$ must be a function of $\symbf{z}$ alone. Then a correct model has $\bar m_m = 0$ in \alert{every} direction.
\item The raw markup difference fails: $\eta^m$ depends on prices and shares, hence on $\omega$, so $\E[\omega^m\, \Delta\eta] \neq 0$ even under true conduct. Its \alert{projection} onto $\symbf{z}$ is valid.
\item $\mathsf{T}_{RV} < 0$ favors $m_1$. Which $g$? The one that best tells $m_1$ from $m_2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The Wrong Model Omits a Markup Difference}
\small
Write $\Delta\eta \equiv \eta^{m_1} - \eta^{m_2}$. Implied costs differ by the markup difference with the opposite sign, so
\begin{align*}
MC^{m_1} - MC^{m_2} = -\Delta\eta, \qquad
h_s^{m_1} - h_s^{m_2} = -\E[\Delta\eta \mid x], \qquad
\omega^{m_1} - \omega^{m_2} = -\big(\Delta\eta - \E[\Delta\eta \mid x]\big).
\end{align*}
Project onto $\symbf{z}$ (using $x \subseteq \symbf{z}$) to get the \alert{discriminating direction}:
\begin{align*}
d(\symbf{z}) \equiv \mu^{m_1} - \mu^{m_2} = -\big(\E[\Delta\eta \mid \symbf{z}] - \E[\Delta\eta \mid x]\big) \equiv -\,g(\symbf{z}).
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item If $m_1$ is true, $\omega^{m_2} = \omega^{m_1} + (\Delta\eta - \E[\Delta\eta \mid x])$: misspecifying conduct puts an \alert{omitted variable}, the residualized markup difference, into the cost shock. The test looks for it.
\item $g$ is the predicted markup difference net of the controls: two regressions of $\Delta\eta$, one on $\symbf{z}$ and one on $x$, differenced (Frisch--Waugh--Lovell / Robinson).
\item Subtracting $\E[\Delta\eta \mid x]$ cancels exactly the difference between the two models' cost functions.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why $g$ Is the Optimal Single Direction}
\small
Collapse the difference in cost shocks against an arbitrary direction $b(\symbf{z})$:
\begin{align*}
M(b) \equiv \E\big[(\omega^{m_1} - \omega^{m_2})\, b(\symbf{z})\big] = \langle d, b \rangle,
\qquad
\text{s.e.}\big(\widehat M(b)\big) = \frac{\sigma\, \|b\|}{\sqrt n} \ \text{ when } \Var(\omega^{m_1} - \omega^{m_2} \mid \symbf{z}) = \sigma^2 .
\end{align*}
Choose $b$ to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio. Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\langle d, b \rangle \leq \|d\|\, \|b\|$, with equality iff $b \propto d$, so
\begin{align*}
\frac{\sqrt{n}\, \langle d, b \rangle}{\sigma\, \|b\|} \;\leq\; \frac{\sqrt{n}\, \|d\|}{\sigma} = \frac{\sqrt{n}\, \|\mu^{m_1} - \mu^{m_2}\|}{\sigma},
\qquad \text{with equality iff } b \propto d \propto g .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unique}: any direction not proportional to $g$ has strictly less power to tell the two models apart.
\item \textbf{Attains the full CMR}: the best single direction recovers the whole conditional-moment distance $\|\mu^{m_1} - \mu^{m_2}\|$.
\item \textbf{Chamberlain (1987)}: on the line $\eta(\tau) = \tau\eta^{m_1} + (1-\tau)\eta^{m_2}$, $\E[\partial\omega(\tau)/\partial\tau \mid \symbf{z}] = -g$, so $g$ is the optimal instrument for $\tau$ (with heteroskedasticity, $g/\sigma^2(\symbf{z})$).
\item \textbf{Caveats}: optimal \alert{per pair} (each pair has its own $g$), and direction is not strength: $z^e$ must still predict $\Delta\eta$ beyond $x$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Rivers--Vuong on $g$ Reproduces the Full-CMR Ranking}
\small
Optimality was for the linear contrast $\bar m_1 - \bar m_2$; Rivers--Vuong uses squares. Both factor through the same inner product:
\begin{align*}
\Delta\widetilde Q(b) = (\bar m_1 - \bar m_2)(\bar m_1 + \bar m_2) = \underbrace{\langle d, b \rangle}_{\text{discrimination}}\; \underbrace{\langle r, b \rangle}_{\text{level}},
\qquad r \equiv \mu^{m_1} + \mu^{m_2} .
\end{align*}
Normalize $\hat g = g/\|g\| = -d/\|d\|$. Then $\langle d, \hat g \rangle = -\|d\|$, $\langle r, \hat g \rangle = -\langle d, r \rangle / \|d\|$, and
\begin{align*}
\Delta\widetilde Q(\hat g) = \langle d, r \rangle = \langle \mu^{m_1} - \mu^{m_2},\, \mu^{m_1} + \mu^{m_2} \rangle = \|\mu^{m_1}\|^2 - \|\mu^{m_2}\|^2 = Q(m_1) - Q(m_2).
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item In population the scalar test and the full CMR \alert{rank the models identically and share the null}: nothing is lost by using one direction.
\item Population only: in samples $\widehat g$ is estimated, so bias, variance and first-stage rates differ from the full-CMR estimator (BCS 2.0).
\item Maximizing the raw $\Delta\widetilde Q(b)$ instead would tilt $b$ toward the bisector of $d$ and $r$: a bigger statistic for a different null (equal projection, not equal fit).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{When Does the Test Fail?}
\footnotesize
The RV variance degenerates exactly when \alert{neither} model is falsified in the direction used, $\bar m_1 = \bar m_2 = 0$ (DMSS Proposition 3).\\[0.15cm]
\textbf{Two failures survive the optimal $g$}; both are matters of identification, not of instrument choice:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Equal conditional moments}, $d = 0$: $g$ itself vanishes and there is nothing to rank. If $\mu^{m_1} \equiv \mu^{m_2} \equiv 0$ (both correct, or $z^e$ irrelevant for both), this is the DMSS degeneracy. With a common misfit the limit is non-degenerate, but $\widehat g$ is pure first-stage noise and the fixed-$\widehat g$ SE is too small (9--15\% rejections at nominal 5\%).
\item \textbf{Proportional moments}, $\mu^{m_1} \propto \mu^{m_2}$. Canonical case: two constant-markup rules $p = a_m\, mc$ give $\mu^m = v/a_m$ with $v = \E[p \mid \symbf{z}] - \E[p \mid x]$, so $\Delta\widetilde Q = \|v\|^2 (a_1^{-2} - a_2^{-2})$ and the test consistently picks the \alert{larger} markup: scale, not fit, and no instrument fixes it. Never compare two constant-markup conducts. Perfect competition is a straw man: ``we reject PC'' only says that prices project onto $z^e$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Two more failures hit a generic direction $b$}, which need not lie in $\text{span}\{\mu^{m_1}, \mu^{m_2}\}$:
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{2}
\item $\langle d, b \rangle = 0$ with $d \neq 0$: $\bar m_1 = \bar m_2$, spurious equal fit, no power.
\item $b \perp \text{span}\{\mu^{m_1}, \mu^{m_2}\}$: $\bar m_1 = \bar m_2 = 0$, the original Vuong degeneracy.
\end{enumerate}
Along $g \propto d \in \text{span}\{\mu^{m_1}, \mu^{m_2}\}$, (3) and (4) can happen only at $d = 0$. In samples a poor $\widehat g$ (weak $z^e$) can bring them back.
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Plugging In $\widehat h_s$ and $\widehat g$: Orthogonality and Cross-Fitting}
\footnotesize
Both nuisance functions are fit by machine learning and \alert{plugged in once}, never refit in the bootstrap. Why is that safe? Perturb $(h_s, g)$ by $(\delta h, \delta g)$:
\begin{align*}
\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \bar m_m = \underbrace{-\,\E\big[\delta h(x)\, \E[g \mid x]\big]}_{\text{cost direction}} + \underbrace{\E\big[\mu^m\, \delta g\big]}_{\text{instrument direction}},
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \widetilde Q(m) = 2\, \bar m_m \cdot \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \bar m_m .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cost direction: zero by construction.} $\E[g \mid x] = 0$ because we subtracted $\E[\Delta\eta \mid x]$ (Neyman orthogonality; Chernozhukov et al.\ 2018). The subtraction is not redundant even though $x \subseteq \symbf{z}$: without it, error in $\widehat h_s$ enters the moment at first order.
\item \textbf{Instrument direction: zero iff $\mu^m \equiv 0$.} A model that satisfies the CMR has its moment pinned at zero however badly $g$ is estimated. With a true model on the menu, $\widehat g$-error moves \alert{power}, not size.
\item \textbf{Both-wrong null} ($\bar m_1 = -\bar m_2 = -\|d\|^2/2$): a term $-\|d\|^2 \langle r, \delta g \rangle$ survives along the level direction, and a fixed-$\widehat g$ SE omits its variance: $\Var(\mathsf{T}_{RV}) \rightarrow 1 + R^2_r\,(1 - R^2_{fs})/R^2_{fs}$, with $R^2_{fs}$ the first-stage $R^2$ of $\Delta\eta$ on $z^e$ net of $x$ and $R^2_r$ the share of the level they explain. Report $R^2_{fs}$. A refit bootstrap restores the term, and the full-CMR criterion (no estimated direction) is immune.
\item \textbf{Cross-fitting} over whole clusters (markets, never products within a market) removes own-observation overfitting. The remaining bias is a product of two nuisance errors, negligible once each converges faster than $n^{-1/4}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Inference for the Scalar Test}
\small
Aggregate to \alert{cluster totals} $S^m_c = \sum_{i \in c} w_i\, \widehat\omega^m_i \widehat g_i$ (clusters are unions of whole markets, $w_i$ observation weights), so $\bar m_m = \frac{1}{n}\sum_c S^m_c$. No method below refits a nuisance function; each reweights the cached totals.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Analytic (default).} Delta method with gradient $(2\bar m_1, -2\bar m_2)$ and a cluster-robust variance. Fails as $\bar m_m \rightarrow 0$, where $\widehat\sigma \rightarrow 0$.
\item \textbf{Multiplier on the influence function.} Rademacher weights $e_c$ on the linearized contributions. Reproduces the analytic SE and its boundary problem (Kline--Santos 2012).
\item \textbf{Score-level wild.} Perturb each moment \alert{before squaring}, $\bar m^*_m = \bar m_m + \frac{1}{n}\sum_c e_c \tilde S^m_c$ with $\tilde S^m_c$ the centered totals, then square. The extra quadratic term takes over near the boundary, so the bootstrap law self-adapts with no pretest. Recenter, since squaring a mean biases it up: report $\Delta\widetilde Q - (\widehat V_1 - \widehat V_2)$, with $\widehat V_m$ the bootstrap variance of $\bar m^*_m$.
\item \textbf{Wild cluster bootstrap-$t$.} Studentize each draw: the few-cluster refinement (Cameron--Gelbach--Miller 2008).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Algorithm (BCS 1.0)}
\small
For a pair $(m_1, m_2)$; the menu test repeats over all pairs.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Split.} Partition the $C$ clusters into $K$ folds; every observation inherits its cluster's fold, and every first stage reuses the split.
\item \textbf{Cost residuals, per model.} For each $m$ and fold $k$, fit $\widehat h_s^{m,(-k)}(x) = \widehat{\E}[MC^m \mid x]$ on the other folds (fixed effects absorbed by a within-transform) and set $\widehat\omega^m_i = MC^m_i - \widehat h_s^{m,(-k)}(x_i)$. Computed once per model and reused in every pair.
\item \textbf{Instrument, per pair.} $\widehat g_i = \widehat{\E}^{(-k)}[\Delta\eta \mid \symbf{z}]_i - \widehat{\E}^{(-k)}[\Delta\eta \mid x]_i$. Regress $\Delta\eta$ itself: for a nonlinear learner, $\widehat{\E}[\eta^{m_1} \mid \cdot] - \widehat{\E}[\eta^{m_2} \mid \cdot] \neq \widehat{\E}[\Delta\eta \mid \cdot]$.
\item \textbf{Moments.} Cluster totals $S^m_c$, moments $\bar m_m$, contrast $\Delta\widetilde Q = \bar m_1^2 - \bar m_2^2$.
\item \textbf{Inference.} $\mathsf{T}_{RV}$ with the analytic cluster SE; score-level wild or bootstrap-$t$ near the boundary. $\mathsf{T}_{RV} < 0$ favors $m_1$.
\item \textbf{Menu.} Pairwise matrix of contrasts $\rightarrow$ model confidence set: the models never significantly beaten.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{The full conditional-moment test (BCS 2.0)}

\begin{frame}{The Full Conditional-Moment Test in One Slide (BCS 2.0)}
\small
Use all of $\mu^m(\symbf{z}) = \E[\omega^m \mid \symbf{z}]$, not one direction: $Q(m) = \|\mu^m\|^2$, $H_0: Q(m_1) = Q(m_2)$. The scalar BCS moment, the DMSS 2SLS quadratic form, and this norm are one direction, $K$ directions, and all directions of the same object.
\begin{align*}
\widehat{Q}(m) = \frac{1}{C(C-1)} \sum_{c \neq c'} (u^m_c)' u^m_{c'}, \qquad u^m_c = \widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2} \sum_{i \in c} \widehat{\omega}^m_{i}\, b(z^e_{i}), \qquad b \text{ a series basis, controls partialled out}.
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cross-cluster U-statistic}: products of residuals from different markets are unbiased for $\|\mu^m\|^2$; the own-cluster terms carry the estimation noise that inflates any plug-in (the ``positive shift''), so they are deleted.
\item \textbf{Why bother}: the degenerate set is the \alert{same} as the scalar test's (neither model falsified), so it buys no power there. What it buys is an instrument side with \alert{no estimated direction}: where the scalar $\widehat{g}$ is first-stage noise (equal conditional moments, the both-wrong null) the norm keeps nominal size. One cross-fit regression per model, nothing refit per pair.
\item \textbf{The price}: under a correct model the criterion sits at its minimum and is not normal, so inference has two regimes (two slides on).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The Full-CMR Procedure: Which Regressions, and How the Criterion Is Formed}
\footnotesize
For a menu of models $m$; only step 2 is per model, nothing is refit per pair.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Split.} Partition the $C$ clusters (whole markets) into $K$ folds; the same split serves every regression below.
\item \textbf{Cost residuals, per model.} Regress $MC^m = p - \eta^m$ on the controls $x$ with the FE absorbed by a within-transform, out of fold: $\widehat{\omega}^m_i = MC^m_i - \widehat{h}_s^{m,(-k)}(x_i)$. The same residuals as the scalar test; the only nonparametric nuisance.
\item \textbf{Instrument basis.} Take a series basis $b(z^e) \in \mathbb{R}^{K_z}$ on the excluded instruments (a low-order polynomial). Regress every column on $x$, cross-fit, and keep the residuals $\tilde{b}(z^e)$: the controls are partialled out of the basis exactly as they are out of $\widehat{g}$ (mean-centering is the constant-only case). Whiten with $\widehat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde{b}_i \tilde{b}_i'$.
\item \textbf{The CMR estimate is a regression.} Regress $\widehat{\omega}^m$ on $\tilde{b}$: $\widehat{\mu}^m = \tilde{b}'\,\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}\, \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde{b}_i \widehat{\omega}^m_i$, the fitted value. Its mean square, $\widehat{\nu}_m' \widehat{\nu}_m$ with $\widehat{\nu}_m = \widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2} \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde{b}_i \widehat{\omega}^m_i$, is the 2SLS quadratic form of DMSS. It is biased upward by the estimation variance of $\widehat{\mu}^m$.
\item \textbf{Debias with cluster scores.} $G^m_c = \sum_{i \in c} w_i\, \widehat{\omega}^m_i \tilde{b}_i$, $u^m_c = \widehat{\Sigma}^{-1/2} G^m_c$, and
$\widehat{Q}(m) = \frac{1}{C(C-1)}\big(\|\sum_c u^m_c\|^2 - \sum_c \|u^m_c\|^2\big)$: the cross-cluster products only. Dropping $c = c'$ drops $\E\|\widehat{\mu}^m - \mu^m\|^2$.
\item \textbf{Contrast and inference.} $\Delta\widehat{Q} = \widehat{Q}(m_1) - \widehat{Q}(m_2)$; per-cluster influence $\widehat{\psi}_c$ (leave-one-out $\widehat{\nu}$, weight-matrix term); unified multiplier bootstrap on the cached $u^m_c$; $p$-value from the bootstrap law (next slide).
\item \textbf{Menu.} Pairwise matrix of contrasts $\rightarrow$ model confidence set.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Inference in One Slide: Two Regimes, One Bootstrap}
\footnotesize
\begin{align*}
\Delta\widehat{Q} - \big(Q(m_1) - Q(m_2)\big) = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{C} \textstyle\sum_c \psi_c}_{\sqrt{C}\text{-normal if } \sigma^2_{\text{lin}} > 0} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{C(C-1)} \textstyle\sum_{c \neq c'} \Psi(c,c')}_{\text{rate-}C\text{ weighted } \chi^2 \text{ if } \mu^{m_1} \equiv \mu^{m_2} \equiv 0}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-degenerate}: at least one model violates the CMR on the span of $z^e$; cluster-robust RV is fine. \textbf{Degenerate}: neither does (both correct, or weak instruments for testing); the linear term vanishes, $C\widehat{Q} \rightarrow \sum_r v_r(\chi^2_{1,r} - 1)$, mean zero and right-skewed, so a normal cutoff under-covers the upper tail. The standard error differs by $\sqrt{C}$ between regimes and weak-instrument comparisons sit near the boundary. Because $\widehat{\Sigma}$ is estimated, $\psi_c$ carries a weight-matrix term: per observation the influence of $Q(m)$ is $2v\omega^m - v^2 - Q(m)$ with $v$ the fitted moment (DMSS eq.~8); dropping $v^2$ overstates the variance.
\item \textbf{One multiplier bootstrap} on both terms, $\Delta\widehat{Q}^* - \Delta\widehat{Q} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_c e_c \widehat{\psi}_c + \frac{1}{C(C-1)}\sum_{c \neq c'} e_c e_{c'} \widehat{\Psi}(c,c')$, one Rademacher weight per cluster shared across terms and models. It nests the linear RV and the score-wild bootstrap and interpolates between them with no pretest and no switch; the $p$-value comes from the bootstrap law, not a normal table; nothing is refit.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics}: $\widehat{F} = \max_m C\widehat{Q}(m)$ over the pair (near degeneracy only when \alert{both} criteria are near zero) and the first-stage fit of $\Delta\eta$ on $z^e$ (is there anything to rank?), both descriptive. \textbf{Open}: uniform validity across the boundary (the Shi 2015 analogue), demand-estimation uncertainty, a Hansen model confidence set.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[plain]{The Reduced-Form Literature (and its Critics)}
\small
Symmetric homogeneous-good Cournot with profit weights gives a share-weighted average markup (with market demand elasticity $\epsilon$):
\begin{align*}
\sum_f s_f \cdot \frac{p - mc_f}{p} = \frac{1}{\epsilon} \underbrace{\symbf{s}' \, \mathcal{H}(\kappa) \, \symbf{s}}_{\equiv MHHI}, \qquad
MHHI\Delta \equiv MHHI - \underbrace{\symbf{s} \cdot \symbf{s}}_{HHI}
\end{align*}
Some regress
\begin{align*}
\log \left(p_{r j t}\right)=\beta_1 \cdot M H H I \Delta_{r t}+\beta_2 \cdot H H I_{r t}+\theta \cdot X_{r j t}+\alpha_t+\nu_{r j}+u_{r j t}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item Azar, Schmalz, Tecu (JF 2018): airline routes with more common ownership have 3--7\% higher fares.
\item But $MHHI\Delta$ is a function of \alert{equilibrium market shares} -- endogenous! (O'Brien Waehrer 2017). Price-concentration regressions all over again.
\item Dennis, Gerardi, Schnitzler (JF 2022): not robust (driven by the share component); Kennedy, O'Brien, Song, Waehrer (2017): structural version finds nothing.
\item Better idea: treat CO vs.\ own-profit maximization as a \alert{conduct testing} problem.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The Menu Is an Internalization Ladder}
In cereal the candidate conducts differ in \alert{how much of the cross-price structure} the firm prices on:
\begin{align*}
\underbrace{\text{single-product}}_{\text{own elasticities only}} \;\prec\; \underbrace{\text{own-profit}}_{\text{Nash--Bertrand, own firm}} \;\prec\; \underbrace{\text{common ownership } \kappa}_{\text{own firm + profit weights on rivals}} \;\prec\; \underbrace{\text{monopoly}}_{\text{everything}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}
\item \alert{All} of them, single-product included, price above marginal cost. The test ranks models by the recapture they internalize, not by whether market power exists.
\item So ``single-product fits the moment best'' is a statement about \alert{cross-price internalization}, not a rejection of market power, and not a statement that firms ignore their sister brands in any literal sense.
\item Perfect competition is on the menu as a straw man only (the constant-markup degeneracy). Cournot vs.\ Bertrand would be a different ladder.
\item This is the substantive content of the criterion $Q(m) = \|\E[\omega^m \mid \symbf{z}]\|^2$ here: it orders the ladder; it does not estimate a markup level. The markup level comes from demand.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Near-Collinear Markups Lower Power; Instrument Strength Sets the Regime}
\begin{itemize}
\item Across the menu the implied markups are $\geq 0.92$ correlated, so $\Delta\eta = \eta^{m_1} - \eta^{m_2}$ is small relative to $\eta^m$. That does \alert{not} put the test near the degeneracy boundary: the scale of $\Delta\eta$ cancels from the studentized statistic. It lowers power, because the misfit a wrong model adds is small relative to the noise in the cost residual.
\item What the test needs is that the excluded $z$ predict $\Delta\eta$ net of the controls: \alert{instrument strength for conduct}, a different object from strength for demand. An instrument can move prices and shares beautifully and project onto $\Delta\eta$ not at all.
\item Monte Carlo with one model correct: rejection $1.00 \rightarrow 0.05$ as $\text{corr}(\eta^{m_1}, \eta^{m_2})$ rises from $0.97$ to $0.999$. That is power falling as the wrong model's violation shrinks, with both moments going to zero, not a degeneracy caused by collinear markups.
\item Practical implication: before testing, regress $\Delta\eta$ on the instruments (net of controls) and report the fit, the effective first-stage $F$. If nothing explains the markup difference beyond the cost shifters, no test, scalar or norm, will rank the pair. This is the DMSS weak-instruments-for-testing diagnostic in plain clothes, and for the scalar test the same $R^2_{fs}$ sets how far its fixed-$\widehat{g}$ SE is off.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Validity Is Orthogonality to the Cost Shock, Not to Price}
\footnotesize
A conduct instrument must be excluded from $\omega$; it \alert{must} correlate with price, since a useful instrument moves markups. Choosing price-orthogonal instruments manufactures weak ones. The cereal instrument groups, after partialling the controls $x$ (FE included) out:

\toprule

\begin{itemize}
\item The optimal IV is not circular: demand was estimated \alert{without} a supply side, so $\widehat{\E}[p \mid \symbf{z}]$ is a reduced-form fit, not an equilibrium price under an assumed conduct. Its near-perfect span of expected prices is the source of its power.
\item The BLP differentiation instruments are built for demand. Their strength for conduct is a separate empirical question, answered by the first-stage $F$ for $\Delta\eta$ and not by their strength for demand.
\item Partial the controls out of the instrument basis (FWL), as the scalar test partials $\widehat{g}$; otherwise control overlap inflates the Gram matrix and groups are not comparable. Partialling \alert{sharpens} valid strong instruments and exposes FE-collinear ones as weak.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Fixed Effects, Weighting, and the Specification of $h_s$}
\begin{itemize}
\item Fixed effects in $h_s$ absorb cost heterogeneity that would otherwise read as moment violation, and they absorb each instrument's FE-collinear variation: the chain-level demographics and the product-level grain prices lose most of theirs. Product fixed effects matter most.
\item Example: across a 20-cell sweep (5 FE sets $\times$ 2 weightings $\times$ 2 instrument groups) the model confidence set is $\{\text{single}\}$ in every cell with product FE; without product FE and without weights the \alert{collusive} models survive under the optimal IV.
\item Servings weighting roughly halves the criterion magnitudes. Additive versus interacted FE can flip a pairwise decision; the full product$+$year$+$chain set is invariant.
\item Lesson: the \alert{specification} of $h_s$ (what is a cost control, which FE, how flexible, weighted or not) is as much a part of the identification argument as the instruments. Report it, every time.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Single-Product Puzzle}
\begin{itemize}
\item Single-product pricing (each brand ignores its sister brands) fits the moment condition \alert{better} than own-profit maximization. Robust across instruments, samples, fixed effects, weighting, learners, and both criteria; look back at the scalar-test table.
\item It is \alert{not} ``no market power'': single-product markups are $1/(\alpha(1-\sigma_j))$-sized, comfortably above cost. It says firms appear to internalize \alert{less} cross-brand recapture than Bertrand with the estimated demand implies.
\item Candidate explanations, none settled:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Demand misspecification: the cross-price elasticities (recapture) are mismeasured, so the ``correct'' internalization looks wrong through the lens of this demand system. Recall the micro-data week: substitution patterns are the thing BLP gets least right.
\item Rule-of-thumb pricing: brand managers price their own brand; the corporate parent does not reprice Cheerios when Lucky Charms moves.
\item Another instrument or moment may reorder single vs.\ own-profit; nothing we have tried does.
\end{enumerate}
\item Methodological point: a result like this only becomes visible once the test is honest about ranking misspecified models. A specification test would have rejected everything and said nothing.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}[plain]{Stepping Back: What About the Mechanism?}
\begin{itemize}
\item In RTE cereal, the evidence favors own-profit maximization over common ownership pricing, on BCS 1.0 and BCS 2.0 alike.
\item In some sense this is a \alert{frictionless} model
\begin{itemize}
  \item Managers do exactly what investment managers want
  \item Investment managers seek to maximize \alert{portfolio value}
\end{itemize}
\item Some attempts at micro foundations are questionable
\begin{itemize}
  \item Anton, Ederer, Gine, Schmalz: toy model has manager choose effort to reduce $mc$.
  \item Weaker managerial incentives: higher $mc$ and higher $p$
  \item Some correlation that executives at commonly owned firms have performance less correlated with profitability.
\end{itemize}
\item In some sense CO is what we would see absent agency problems, so where are they?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
