\section*{The Model}
Each period Harold Zurcher observes the mileage bin $x_t \in \{0,1,\ldots,89\}$ of a bus and payoff shocks $\varepsilon_t(d)$, which are i.i.d.\ type I extreme value, and chooses $d_t \in \{0,1\}$: keep the engine ($d_t = 0$) or replace it ($d_t = 1$). The flow payoffs are
\begin{align*}
u(x,0;\theta) = -c(x;\theta^c), \qquad u(x,1;\theta) = -RC - c(0;\theta^c), \qquad c(x;\theta^c) = \theta^c_1 \left(\frac{x}{100}\right) + \theta^c_2 \left(\frac{x}{100}\right)^2,
\end{align*}
plus $\varepsilon_t(d_t)$, so $\theta = (\theta^c_1, \theta^c_2, RC, \theta^f)$. We use \emph{standard timing}: $d_t$ is chosen at the recorded state $x_t$, and
\begin{align*}
x_{t+1} = (1-d_t)\, x_t + \Delta_{t+1}, \qquad \Delta_{t+1} \in \{0,1,2,3,4\} \text{ i.i.d.\ with } \Pr(\Delta = k) = \theta^f_k,
\end{align*}
where mileage that would pass bin 89 stays at 89. Throughout, fix the discount factor at $\beta = 0.975$. Because a replacement restarts the bus at zero, $f(x' \mid x,1) = f(x' \mid 0,0)$ and $EV(x,1) = EV(0,0)$, so a single function $EV(x) \equiv EV(x,0)$ on the grid suffices (dropping Euler's $\gamma$):
\begin{align*}
EV(x) = \sum_{x'} f(x' \mid x,0) \ln\left[ e^{v(x',0)} + e^{v(x',1)} \right], \qquad v(x,0) = u(x,0;\theta) + \beta EV(x), \qquad v(x,1) = u(x,1;\theta) + \beta EV(0).
\end{align*}

\section*{Part 0: Pre-Estimation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Estimate $\theta^f$ -- \textbf{you should have 5 of them}. Every period after the first reveals an increment, $\Delta_{i,t+1} = x_{i,t+1} - (1-d_{it})\,x_{it}$.
\item Populate the $90 \times 90$ transition matrices $\mathbf F_0$ (keep) and $\mathbf F_1$ (replace: every row equals row 0 of $\mathbf F_0$). Adjust them so that rows sum to one!
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Part 1: Estimation MLE}
Estimate $(\theta^c_1, \theta^c_2, RC)$ using the NFXP approach of Rust. Computers are fast enough that you can probably get away with finite differences for the gradient, but analytic gradients will be more reliable in addition to faster. If you are feeling ambitious, \texttt{JAX}'s autodiff makes the gradient cheap -- differentiate the fixed point implicitly, not through the iterations (see \texttt{jax.lax.custom\_root}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute $EV(x;\theta)$ for a given guess of the parameters via the fixed point.
\item Construct the CCP $P(1 \mid x;\theta)$ given your $EV(x;\theta)$ for every possible mileage state.
\item Construct the log-likelihood and its gradient with respect to $\theta$ for every possible mileage state (the implicit function theorem gives $\partial EV/\partial\theta = [\mathbf I - \partial T_\theta/\partial EV]^{-1}\, \partial T_\theta/\partial\theta$).
\item Apply your likelihood and gradient contributions to the observed data and maximize. Report standard errors from the inverse Hessian. What do these standard errors ignore?
\item Why can't we estimate $\beta$ along with the other parameters? Re-estimate the model for each $\beta \in \{0.9,\allowbreak 0.95,\allowbreak 0.975,\allowbreak 0.99,\allowbreak 0.999\}$ and report the maximized log-likelihood and $\hat\theta$ for each.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Part 3: The Optimization-Free (CCP) Estimator}
This is adapted from Han Hong's problem set at Stanford: the idea is that we can use the arguments in Hotz and Miller (1993), or Pesendorfer and Schmidt-Dengler (2008), to construct an optimization-free method to recover the payoffs in the Rust problem. Use \textbf{replacement as the reference action}: normalize $u(x,1) = 0$ for all $x$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Use your $\hat{\mathbf F}_0, \hat{\mathbf F}_1$ from Part 0.
\item Estimate the CCP $\hat P(1 \mid x)$ non-parametrically. You can use a binomial logit on a polynomial basis in $x$ (increasing number of terms) or a kernel. Try at least two specifications (e.g.\ quadratic and cubic) and plot them against the empirical replacement frequencies and the CCPs implied by your NFXP estimates. (Extra Credit: Impose monotonicity on the CCPs.)
\item Use the Hotz--Miller inversion: $\hat v(x,0) - \hat v(x,1) = \ln \hat P(0 \mid x) - \ln \hat P(1 \mid x)$.
\item With $u(x,1) = 0$,
\begin{align*}
v(x,1) = \beta \sum_{x'} f(x' \mid x,1)\, V(x') = \beta \sum_{x'} f(x' \mid 0,0) \left[ v(x',1) - \ln \hat P(1 \mid x') \right],
\end{align*}
where $V(x') = \ln \sum_d e^{v(x',d)}$ is the ex ante value function. Show that $v(x,1)$ does not depend on $x$ and solve this fixed point in closed form. Add it to $\hat v(x,0) - \hat v(x,1)$ to recover both choice-specific value functions.
\item Recover the payoff of keeping,
\begin{align*}
\hat u(x,0) = \hat v(x,0) - \beta \sum_{x'} \hat f(x' \mid x,0) \ln\left[ e^{\hat v(x',0)} + e^{\hat v(x',1)} \right].
\end{align*}
Under this normalization $u(x,0) = RC - c(x;\theta^c)$, so a (weighted) regression of $\hat u(x,0)$ on $(1, -x/100, -(x/100)^2)$ over the states you observe recovers $(RC, \theta^c_1, \theta^c_2)$. (Consistent with the displays above, we have dropped the Euler constant $\gamma \approx 0.5772$ from the expected maximum.)
\item This estimator requires only one (trivial) fixed point, so we can do inference by the bootstrap. Compute bootstrap standard errors by resampling \emph{buses} with replacement ($B \geq 200$), re-estimating both first stages ($\hat\theta^f$ and $\hat P$) in every replication. Do this for each first-stage specification. How do the standard errors compare across specifications and with NFXP's, and why?
\item Compare the estimates from all three estimators (NFXP, MPEC, CCP) in a table and discuss. Since $\theta^c_1$ and $\theta^c_2$ are hard to separate, also plot $\hat c(x)$ over the observed mileage range for each estimator.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Part 4: Counterfactuals and the Normalization}
Rust (1987) used his estimates to trace out a demand curve for bus engines. Treat the CCPs implied by your NFXP estimates, $P(1 \mid x;\hat\theta)$ on the whole grid, as the population CCPs.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Recover payoffs from these CCPs by the Hotz--Miller inversion under two normalizations: (R) $u(x,1) = 0$, as in Part 3; and (K) $u(x,0) = 0$, for which
\begin{align*}
V = (\mathbf I - \beta \mathbf F_0)^{-1} \left[-\ln P(0 \mid \cdot)\right], \qquad u(x,1) = V(x) + \ln P(1 \mid x) - \beta (\mathbf F_1 V)(x).
\end{align*}
Verify that both payoff vectors reproduce the same CCPs. Show that (R) differs from the NFXP payoffs by a constant, and plot the (K) payoff $u(x,1)$. Compare it to $-RC + C(x) - C(0)$, where $C = (\mathbf I - \beta \mathbf F_0)^{-1} c$ is the present value of maintenance costs if the engine is never replaced.
\item \textbf{Engine demand.} Let the price of an engine change by $\tau$, so $u^\tau(x,1) = u(x,1) - \tau$. For $\tau \in [-5, 5]$, solve the model, compute the stationary distribution $\mu$ of mileage under the controlled transition matrix
\begin{align*}
\mathbf M = \mathrm{diag}\big(P(0 \mid \cdot)\big)\,\mathbf F_0 + \mathrm{diag}\big(P(1 \mid \cdot)\big)\,\mathbf F_1, \qquad \mu' = \mu' \mathbf M,
\end{align*}
and the long-run engine demand $\bar d(\tau) = \sum_x \mu(x)\, P(1 \mid x)$ (replacements per bus per period). Plot $\bar d(\tau)$ for the NFXP payoffs and for (R) and (K).
\item Now suppose the buses move to routes on which mileage accrues twice as fast: $\Delta' = 2\Delta$, with the same $\hat\theta^f$. Redraw the three demand curves.
\item Which of these two counterfactuals is identified from the CCPs, and which is not? Explain why (R) and (K) agree in 2 but not in 3, and which normalization gives the right answer in 3 and why. (Kalouptsidi, Scott, and Souza-Rodrigues, 2021.)
\item Why didn't we use the first-stage $\hat P$ from Part 3 in this part? Try the counterfactual in 3 at $\tau = 0$ with (R) payoffs recovered from each of your Part 3 first stages, and explain what you find.
\end{enumerate}
